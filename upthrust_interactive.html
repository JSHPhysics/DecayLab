Where Does Upthrust Come From?

A guided derivation — answer each question to unlock the next step

Formulas you already know:
ΔP = ρ · g · Δh  —  pressure increases with depth   |   P = F / A  —  so  F = P · A
Step 1 — The Setup

Pressure acts on the faces of a submerged box

Consider a solid box held in water. The fluid pushes on every face. We focus on the top and bottom horizontal faces, because those are the ones that create a vertical force.

The box on the left is partially submerged (top face is in air). The box on the right is fully submerged.

Box A — partially submerged
Box B — fully submerged

For Box B (fully submerged), the fluid pushes up on the bottom and down on the top. Why is the upward force bigger?

Step 2 — Writing Down the Pressures

Using ΔP = ρgΔh to find the pressure difference

Focus on Box B (fully submerged). Its top face is at depth h₁ below the surface and its bottom face is at depth h₁ + h, where h is the height of the box.

Using ΔP = ρ · g · Δh, the extra pressure at the bottom face compared to the top face is:

ΔP = ρ · g · [(h₁ + h) − h₁]
ΔP = ρ · g · h

The pressure difference only depends on h — the height of the box in the fluid. The depth of the top face h₁ cancels out completely.

If you lower Box B deeper into the water (so h₁ gets larger), what happens to the pressure difference between its bottom and top faces?

▼
Step 3 — From Pressure to Force

Net upward force = pressure difference × area

The fluid exerts an upward force on the bottom and a downward force on the top. Both faces have the same area A. Rearranging P = F/A:

Fbottom = Pbottom · A      Ftop = Ptop · A

Net upward force = Fbottom − Ftop
= (Pbottom − Ptop) · A

= ΔP · A

Upthrust U = ρ · g · h · A

Now notice: h × A is the height of the box multiplied by its cross-sectional area — that is exactly the volume of the box (or for a partial submersion, the volume of the submerged part).

h · A = Vsubmerged

For Box A (partially submerged), there is no downward fluid force on its top face. How should we find the upthrust in that case?

▼
Step 4 — Archimedes' Principle

Upthrust = weight of fluid displaced

Substituting h · A = Vsubmerged into our expression:

U = ρ · g · h · A

U = ρ · g · Vsubmerged

But ρ × Vsubmerged is the mass of fluid that would fill the submerged space — in other words, the mass of fluid displaced by the object. And mass × g is weight, so:

U = weight of fluid displaced

This is Archimedes' Principle — and we derived it directly from pressure!

Two objects have the same volume but different densities. Both are fully submerged in water. Which experiences the greater upthrust?

🎉

Derivation Complete!

Starting only from pressure increases with depth and F = PA, we showed that the net upward force on any submerged object is:

U = ρfluid · g · Vsubmerged

The upthrust arises because the bottom face is always deeper than the top face — so the fluid pushes up harder than it pushes down. The deeper you go, the greater the pressure, but the difference only depends on how tall the submerged portion is.
